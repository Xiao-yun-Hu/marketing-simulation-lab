Research, Calibration & Known Limits

Simulation proposes; reality decides. This page is an honest account of what we've measured internally, what the external published research says about LLM-simulated consumer behavior, and specifically where that evidence says simulation output should not be trusted on its own. Every number here is time-stamped, sourced and scoped — none are reported as measured real-world conversion results.

What the published research says

Calibration against real respondents

Kaiser, Kaiser, Schallner, Manewitsch & Rau compared GPT-4o "digital twin" responses to real, quota-matched consumers on soft-drink preference questions. Synthetic responses tracked real ones reasonably well at the aggregate/trend level, but were measurably weaker at predicting any one individual's response.

Kaiser et al., "Leaving Insight to Digital Twins? Promise, Progress and Limits of Synthetic Respondents," NIM Marketing Intelligence Review 18(1), 2026. nim.org

Algorithmic fidelity — and its limits

Argyle et al. showed that an LLM conditioned on real respondents' sociodemographic backstories could reproduce fine-grained, demographically-correlated response patterns — what they called "algorithmic fidelity." The same paper is explicit that this comes with "not one bias, but many," not a clean substitute for human samples.

Argyle et al., "Out of One, Many: Using Language Models to Simulate Human Samples," Political Analysis 31(3), 2023. arXiv:2209.06899

Demographic misalignment

Santurkar et al. built OpinionsQA and found substantial misalignment between language-model-reflected opinions and real opinions across roughly 60 US demographic groups — in some cases comparable in size to the gap between Democrats and Republicans on climate change — and the misalignment persisted even after prompting the model to adopt a group's perspective. Older, widowed and highly religious respondents were consistently underrepresented well.

Santurkar et al., "Whose Opinions Do Language Models Reflect?" ICML 2023 (PMLR 202). arXiv:2303.17548

Simulated social behavior

Park et al.'s Generative Agents work showed LLM agents with observation, planning and reflection can produce believable simulated social behavior in a sandbox environment — useful as an architectural reference for how we structure simulated decision-making, though it is a systems paper, not a market-research validation study.

Park et al., "Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior," UIST 2023. arXiv:2304.03442

On discoverability: why this page is structured this way

We structure our content for citability by AI assistants and search systems, following the Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) research from Aggarwal et al. That paper proposes metrics for how visible a source is inside generated answers — not a search-ranking position — and found that adding citations and quotations could meaningfully increase that visibility in their benchmark, with results varying by domain. We apply the same discipline here (clear structure, sourced claims, machine-readable metadata) because it is good practice for any reader, human or model — not because it guarantees any outcome.

Aggarwal et al., "GEO: Generative Engine Optimization," KDD 2024. arXiv:2311.09735

Where we tell teams not to trust simulation alone

  • Final launch or budget decisions — validate with a real experiment first
  • Small, niche or culturally specific populations the underlying model may represent poorly
  • Any claim you plan to publish externally as a measured result — simulated outputs should always be labeled as simulated

FAQ

Is simulated consumer behavior as reliable as a real A/B test?
No. In our own internal notes, direction has aligned with real human responses more often than effect size has. Published research shows LLM-simulated responses track real populations better in aggregate than at the individual level, and can systematically misrepresent some demographic groups. Treat simulation as a prioritization tool, not a substitute for a real test.
Does Marketing Simulation Lab guarantee visibility in AI search answers?
No. We follow Generative Engine Optimization practices described in the GEO research (Aggarwal et al., KDD 2024) — structuring content for citability — but no page structure, schema or llms.txt file can guarantee ranking, citation or recommendation by any AI system.
What are the known limitations of LLM-simulated consumers?
Published research has found LLM-generated opinions can misalign with real demographic groups' views, that simulated responses track real populations better at the aggregate level than the individual level, and that simulated effect sizes can differ from real human effect sizes even when direction matches.
